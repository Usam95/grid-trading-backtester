Pause, range exhaustion, stop, and emergency are different contracts.

You will decide which orders survive, when reconciliation is mandatory, which states resume, and which events permanently end the run.

Run identity

Open-ended does not mean immortal.

A successfully activated run has no preset duration or cycle count. It ends only through an approved terminal condition.

EventTerminal?Same run may continue?
Price leaves rangeNoYes, normal cycling resumes on re-entry.
Exposure-reducing pauseNoYes, after explicit reconciled resume.
Zero current inventoryNoYes, the continuing strategy may buy again.
Operator stopYesNo; future activity needs a new run.
Global stop-lossYesNo; liquidation permanently closes it.

Boundary state

Range-exhausted is still an active run.

The price is outside, but no new exposure is created beyond the configured outer rung.

Price falls below the lower bound

Planned buys may already have filled, so inventory may be near its maximum. No lower buy is invented. Valid in-range sells remain as recovery-side orders and normal cycling resumes if price returns.

Price rises above the upper bound

Planned sells may have reduced inventory. No higher sell is invented. Valid in-range buys remain recovery-side orders and normal cycling resumes if price returns.

Leaving the range does not itself trigger liquidation, pause, range shifting, or global stop-loss. The separately configured stop-loss may still trigger if its own threshold is crossed.

reconciled resume after ten minutes

The operator requests resume. Before rebuilding anything, the engine compares venue orders, all fills including late fills, balances, grid inventory, current price state, refreshed venue filters, and risk limits. It rebuilds only missing valid orders. If price is now below the range, the same run resumes into range-exhausted, not a fresh bootstrap.

Permanent endings

Operator stop is deliberate; emergency stop is defensive.

Both cancel all managed orders and reconcile racing fills, but inventory disposition differs.

Operator stop

Cancel all buys and sells, reconcile responses and late fills, calculate final grid inventory, then choose retain or liquidate. Retain is the default; liquidation needs separate confirmation with estimated costs. The run is closed.

Emergency stop

Block new commands, cancel all managed orders, reconcile authoritative venue state, and retain inventory. Automatic liquidation is unsafe when data, connectivity, or balances may be unreliable.

a fill races with cancellation

A 0.010 BTC sell is being canceled, but 0.004 BTC fills first. Final truth is 0.004 sold and 0.006 canceled. Reconciliation records proceeds, fee asset, and inventory reduction before any retained-holding or liquidation decision. “Cancel requested” is not evidence that nothing executed.

Two kinds of selling

A paired sell cycles; a global stop-loss exits.

They may both sell base, but their intent and lifecycle effect are opposite.

Paired rung sell

Sells net base one rung above its filled buy. It realizes one normal cycle, creates the replacement buy, and keeps the run open.

Global stop-loss

At its configured adverse threshold: block new orders, cancel all, reconcile late fills, aggressively liquidate remaining grid inventory, record actual costs, and permanently close the run.

The lower grid bound is not automatically a stop-loss. Exact threshold form and approval constraints belong to the risk specification.

Existing account

Shared account does not mean shared allocation.

The MVP may use your Binance account, but this grid needs exclusive ownership of its active symbol and reserved assets.

manual BTC sale during a running grid

The account contained 2 BTC before activation; 0.8 BTC was allocated to the grid. A manual sale removes 0.2 BTC. Total account balance cannot reveal whether grid inventory or unrelated holdings were consumed. This is foreign activity: the engine pauses and reconciles rather than guessing ownership.

Concurrent algorithms later need Binance subaccount isolation or a portfolio allocator that owns shared reservations and cross-strategy risk.
